What focus is, and what it does

Focus is not a personality trait. It is a brain state. Your brain has two big networks. One is the task network, which runs whatever is in front of you. The other is the default network, which wanders, daydreams, and asks "what else is there." In most brains they seesaw: when one is on, the other is off.

In an ADHD brain they do not seesaw. Both stay on at once. That is the whole problem. You are working and wandering at the same time, so nothing finishes.

Dopamine is what makes the seesaw work. It is the conductor that says "task network now, default network quiet." Low dopamine means no conductor. That is why attention scatters, why impulses win, and why time feels wrong.

What focus does when it is on: the task network holds the goal, the default network stays quiet, and time reads correctly. Things finish. When it is off, every finished thing feels like it took forever, so you quit before the finish line.

Two more things you should know. Hyperfocus is the ADHD strength. Attention locks hard when a task is concrete and has a clear finish. "Make ad four" is concrete. "Do marketing" is not. And attention has blinks: right after you hit a target, attention shuts off for a beat. ADHD brains blink more. Finishing an ad is a target, so the blink comes right after, and that is when the phone urge shows up.

Last: focus is trained, not found. Your eyes and your attention share a circuit. Holding your eyes still trains the circuit. One 17-minute sitting of steady attention reduced blinks in a study, and the effect lasted.

How to finish all ten

  1. Phone in another room. Over about two hours a day of phone use in adults produces ADHD-like attention loss. Out of reach, not face down.
  2. Say the finish line out loud: "ten ads, one sitting." A concrete finish is what lets hyperfocus lock on.
  3. Sixty seconds before you start. Stare at one point for thirty seconds, blinking as little as feels fine. Then let your eyes go wide and take in the whole room for thirty seconds. This is the eye circuit warming up the attention circuit.
  4. Make ad one. Tap done. The tap matters. A small finish releases dopamine, and dopamine is the conductor for ad two.
  5. When you drift, stare at one point for five seconds, then go back. Do not switch apps to "just check." That is the blink talking.
  6. Around ad five, time will feel slow. That is low dopamine, not the clock. Look at the count, not the feeling. Take thirty seconds of wide gaze and go again.
  7. Ad six through ten, same loop. One ad, tap, one line of science, next ad. Nothing else exists.
  8. After ten, stop. You just did more than seventeen minutes of steady attention on real work. That is the training dose. Tomorrow, a different task, same page.
