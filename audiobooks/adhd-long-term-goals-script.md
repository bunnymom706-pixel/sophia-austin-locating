# Finish What You Start: Long-Term Goals With an ADHD Brain
**Runtime:** ~5 minutes | **Audience:** 22-year-old woman with ADHD chasing goals 2+ months out

---

Welcome. Five minutes. One idea: your ADHD brain is not broken for long-term goals. It is just wired for now instead of later. So we are going to drag later into now, and build a system that carries you when motivation does not.

Before anything else, the foundation. Commit your work to the Lord, and your plans will be established. Proverbs sixteen, verse three. You are not white-knuckling this alone. Start every goal on your knees, not in your planner. That single shift takes the pressure off performance and puts it on obedience, and obedience is a daily-sized thing. Your brain does daily-sized very well.

Tip one. Change the picture before you change the plan. Maxwell Maltz, in Psycho-Cybernetics, found that people snap back to whatever self-image they hold, like a thermostat. If you see yourself as the girl who starts strong and fizzles at week three, you will fizzle at week three, no matter how good your app is. So spend sixty seconds a day, eyes closed, seeing yourself as the woman who already finished. Feel it. Bob Proctor taught the same thing: repetition of an idea, with emotion, impresses it on the subconscious. The subconscious runs the show while your prefrontal cortex is off chasing a squirrel.

Tip two. Write the goal in present tense, and read it twice a day. Morning and night. Out loud. Not "I want to finish," but "I am so happy and grateful now that I have finished." Keep it on one index card in your bag. This is the Proctor Gallagher method, and the reason it works for ADHD is that you do not have to remember your goal. You just have to touch the card. Externalize memory. Always.

Tip three. Shrink the horizon. Two months is invisible to a brain that discounts the future steeply. So stop working toward month two. Work toward Friday. Take the goal and cut it into eight weekly wins, then define only this week's win. One sentence. Put it on your calendar as a real event with a real time block, because if it is not on the calendar, it does not exist. Then the long game becomes eight short games, and you only ever play one.

Tip four. Build the loop, not the willpower. Charles Duhigg: every habit is cue, routine, reward. Pick a cue that already happens without you deciding, like your first coffee, or closing your laptop. Stack the new routine right behind it. Then reward yourself immediately, within seconds. Say it out loud, check the box, play one song. ADHD is a dopamine timing problem more than a discipline problem, so pay yourself now, not in eight weeks.

Tip five. Never miss twice. You will miss. Plan for it. A missed day is data, two missed days is a new identity. So the rule is simple: whatever happened yesterday, you get back on today, even if the version you do is two minutes long. Two minutes protects the self-image, and the self-image is the whole game.

Tip six. Make the target big enough to be interesting. This is the Grant Cardone piece, and it is counterintuitive. Small, sensible goals are boring, and a bored ADHD brain will not show up. Set the target high enough that it actually lights you up, then take massive, obvious action in the first seventy-two hours. Momentum early buys you weeks of compliance later. And even at ten percent of a huge goal, you are further than a hundred percent of a timid one.

Tip seven. Let the environment do the work. Willpower is the most expensive tool you own, and yours gets spent by ten a.m. So make the goal frictionless and the distraction expensive. Phone in another room. Clothes laid out. Tabs closed. One task visible. And use body doubling: work on a call with a friend, or in a coffee shop. Presence of another person borrows you an external prefrontal cortex, and there is no shame in that.

Tip eight. Track it where you can see it. A wall calendar with an X for every day you executed. Not a perfect app, a visible chain. Your brain will not remember the effort you put in three weeks ago, so give it proof. Proof is what turns motivation into identity.

Last thing. Paradigms are just habits of thought, and they were installed in you before you were old enough to consent. Which means they can be replaced. Not by trying harder, but by repetition, awareness, and grace. So do not measure this in eight weeks. Measure it in one obedient day, repeated.

You are not behind. You are early, and you are learning the system now that most people never learn at all.

Now go read your card.
